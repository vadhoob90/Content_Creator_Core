# Core editorial standard

Write and review for meaning, reader value, and the selected author's voice.
Apply these shared defaults to content drafts and revisions. They are editorial
guidance, not an authorship detector or a word/punctuation blacklist.

## Authority and local voice

Explicit author instructions, the selected voice, active learnings, and the
content pack's audience and format specialise these style defaults. Preserve
regional English, specialist terminology, deliberate repetition, and supported
author habits. A terse, formal, exuberant, or dash-rich voice is legitimate.
Core evidence, provenance, and approval boundaries still apply. Where the
author's style is unknown, use clear prose appropriate to the task; do not
invent a personality. Never import another voice's evidence or preferences.

## Drafting and revision

- Start with the actual subject, observation, or reader need. Remove ceremonial
  openings, generic scene-setting, and promises of insight that add no context.
- Prefer specific subjects and verbs. Replace inflated abstractions, stacked
  nominalisations, and promotional adjectives when simpler wording carries the
  meaning. Keep technical terms when they are accurate and useful.
- Give each paragraph a purpose. Remove repeated claims, stock transitions,
  restated conclusions, and calls for engagement that the task does not need.
- Let sentence length and paragraph breaks follow the thought and voice.
  Revise monotonous strings of fragments or similarly shaped sentences when
  they interrupt meaning. Keep short sentences for emphasis or accessibility;
  keep longer sentences when they express a relationship clearly. Do not force
  random variation or impose numerical rhythm targets.
- Use em dashes (—), en dashes (–), and double hyphens (--) deliberately and in
  the author's house style. Revise repeated interruptions when they obscure
  the point. Do not ban or mechanically replace punctuation; preserve quoted
  text, code, ranges, and compound terms.
- Check repeated rhetorical templates: "not X, but Y", question-and-answer
  hooks, symmetrical triples, and dramatic one-line endings. Use them when
  they convey a real distinction or serve the voice; remove empty framing.
- Inspect trailing participial clauses such as "..., highlighting ..." or
  "..., underscoring ..." for unsupported significance or causal implications.
  State the supported relationship directly or remove the inference.
- Match confidence to evidence. Avoid generic praise, exaggerated importance,
  and habitual hedges; retain uncertainty, qualifications, and attribution when
  the evidence requires them.
- Use headings, lists, bold text, and summaries where they help this audience
  navigate this format. Do not add the same scaffold to every piece.
- Read the whole piece for coherence and resemblance to the selected voice.
  Do not add errors, slang, fabricated anecdotes, invented numbers, personal
  feelings, or unsupported detail to make writing appear human. If useful
  specificity needs missing evidence, narrow the claim or report the gap using
  the application's defined response.

## Criticism and author review

Use the existing clarity, reader_value, and voice_authenticity dimensions;
use evidence_integrity for unsupported claims. Keep the existing scoring and
severity definitions. For an editorial issue, identify the passage, explain
the effect on meaning or the reader, and give an actionable revision direction
within the requested critique structure. Do not rewrite the whole piece.
Do not double-count one weakness across dimensions or escalate a cosmetic
preference into a factual-integrity blocker.

A word, dash, short sentence, dialect, or detector score alone is insufficient
grounds for an issue. Do not assign an AI probability, claim human authorship,
optimise for detector evasion, or demand removal of a supported voice feature.
Respect explicit author rejections through the existing issue-disposition
workflow. The author remains the final editorial authority.
